Back to basics

What it is, where the name came from, and why it matters.

Definition~2 min

A syndrome, not a diagnosis

🫁

Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage

Bleeding from the alveolar capillaries: the pulmonary microcirculation

+
🫘

Glomerulonephritis

Usually rapidly progressive (RPGN), often crescentic

=

Pulmonary–Renal Syndrome

A clinical picture with many causes, mostly small-vessel vasculitis or anti-GBM disease.
Saying "PRS" is like saying "breathless": it starts the thinking, it doesn't finish it.

10:00–11:00. Key message: PRS is a pattern. Your job is to name the cause, because treatment depends on it.

Optional aside: in PRS the lung bleeding is usually caused by pulmonary capillaritis. Lung tissue is rarely obtained, so most cases are confirmed on renal biopsy and serology.

History⏩ optional~2 min

A short history of naming things

1919
Goodpasture

An 18-year-old during the influenza pandemic: lung haemorrhage and glomerulonephritis. Ernest Goodpasture describes the post-mortem.

1958
Stanton & Tange

Report nine similar cases and coin "Goodpasture's syndrome". He never asked for it.

1967
Lerner, Glassock & Dixon

Antibodies eluted from human kidneys cause nephritis in monkeys. Anti-GBM disease is proven.

1982
Davies et al.

Anti-neutrophil antibodies found in necrotising GN, in Melbourne. ANCA is born.

1985
van der Woude

Links ANCA to Wegener's granulomatosis, now GPA.

2012
Chapel Hill

Revised nomenclature. Eponyms out, mechanisms in.

📜 Goodpasture never described the antibody. He just had the luck to have a syndrome named after him. Lesson: write up your case reports.

"Wegener's" was dropped (2011) after historians uncovered Friedrich Wegener's Nazi affiliations. Say GPA.

11:00–13:00. Light-hearted slide. Ask the room: "Who here still says Wegener's?" Most consultants will put a hand up. Explain why the name changed.

Terminology: "Goodpasture syndrome" = PRS of any cause (old usage); "Goodpasture disease" / anti-GBM disease = the antibody-mediated disease. Modern practice: say "anti-GBM disease".

Why it matters~1 min

Why should we care?

~1/3

of patients with DAH have no haemoptysis at presentation

25–50%

reported mortality when DAH complicates ANCA vasculitis

~80%

historic 1-year mortality of untreated generalised GPA (median survival about 5 months)

Days

how long active crescents take to scar, after which the kidney doesn't come back

Time is nephrons, and alveoli. This is a medical emergency, on a par with sepsis or STEMI. Treatment can start before every result is back.

13:00–14:00. The "1/3 without haemoptysis" figure is the one to stress for juniors. DAH can look like pulmonary oedema or pneumonia on the CXR.

Historical mortality: Walton, BMJ 1958 (untreated Wegener's: median survival about 5 months, roughly 80% dead within a year). Cyclophosphamide and steroids changed everything (Fauci, NIH, 1970s–80s).

Double-positive disease

About 30–40% of patients with anti-GBM disease are also ANCA-positive, usually MPO.

They present like anti-GBM disease (aggressive) but can relapse like ANCA vasculitis.

So always send both antibodies.

14:00–15:00. AAV is by far the most common cause. The SpR point: always request ANCA and anti-GBM together, because double positivity changes management (it needs plasma exchange like anti-GBM, and maintenance like AAV).

Why the lungs and the kidneys?

Two capillary beds that share the same weak spot.

Section divider. 17:00.

Pathophysiology~2 min

Same target, different address

17:00–19:00. Walk through the picture. Both organs are, in effect, a capillary wrapped around a basement membrane.

  • Alveolus: capillary capillaritis leads to RBCs crossing into the air space, which is DAH.
  • Glomerulus: fibrinoid necrosis lets fibrin and cells into Bowman's space. Epithelial proliferation forms a crescent, which scars within days to weeks.

A good question for SpRs: "Why does anti-GBM disease affect the kidney first, and only some patients' lungs?" Answer on the anti-GBM slide: glomerular endothelium is fenestrated, alveolar endothelium isn't.

Pathophysiology~2 min

ANCA: friendly fire from neutrophils

🛡️ Neutrophils are the friendly-fire specialists of the immune system: well meaning, heavily armed, and pointing the wrong way.

19:00–21:00. Four steps:

  • Priming: infection or cytokines (TNF-α) move PR3/MPO to the neutrophil surface. This is why infections often trigger flares.
  • ANCA binding cross-links surface antigen and Fc receptors, which fully activates the cell.
  • Activation: degranulation, reactive oxygen species, NETs. The alternative complement pathway produces C5a, which recruits and primes more neutrophils. That is the target of avacopan (C5aR1 antagonist).
  • Endothelial necrosis gives capillaritis: DAH in the lung, necrotising crescentic GN in the kidney.

Pauci-immune means very little immunoglobulin is left in the tissue on immunofluorescence, even though antibodies drive the disease.

Pathophysiology~2 min

Anti-GBM: the hidden epitope

  • Target: the NC1 domain of the α3 chain of type IV collagen (and α5)
  • The epitope is normally cryptic, hidden inside the NC1 hexamer
  • Rare: about 1 per million per year. Peaks in young men (often PRS) and in older women (often renal-limited)
  • Lung haemorrhage is strongly linked to smoking and inhaled hydrocarbons
  • Monophasic: relapse is uncommon, unlike AAV

🚬 Another reason to stop smoking: it unlocks the lung for anti-GBM antibodies.

21:00–23:00. The key concept is a hidden epitope that gets exposed. Why is the lung only sometimes involved? Glomerular capillaries are fenestrated, so circulating antibody reaches the GBM. The alveolar BM sits behind continuous endothelium and needs a second injury, classically smoking.

HLA-DR15 is strongly associated. Ask the consultants if anyone has seen a case: most will have seen only one or two in their career.

Note that relapse is rare in anti-GBM disease, so no long-term maintenance is needed in most cases, unless the patient is double-positive.

Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage~2 min

The lung can hide a lot of blood

🩸

Haemoptysis

Ranges from streaks to frank blood. Absent in about 1 in 3.

📉

Falling Hb

An unexplained drop, often 15–20 g/L or more, with no other source.

🌫️

New infiltrates

Bilateral, often perihilar. Can change within hours.

🫁

Hypoxaemia

Out of proportion to the CXR, or rising oxygen needs.

Supporting tests

  • KCO raised by more than 30% suggests a fresh bleed (if the patient is well enough to do the test)
  • BAL: progressively bloodier aliquots, haemosiderin-laden macrophages
  • CT: ground glass and consolidation
~70 m²

Alveolar surface area, roughly a badminton court. Plenty of room to hide a unit of blood without coughing any of it up.

25:00–27:00. The classic tetrad. Stress for juniors: a falling Hb plus new bilateral shadowing should make you think of DAH, with or without haemoptysis.

KCO: blood in the alveoli takes up CO, so the transfer coefficient rises. It is the one condition where an ill patient's KCO goes up. Rarely practical in the acute setting, but useful for recurrent bleeding.

The differential of DAH beyond PRS: coagulopathy, mitral stenosis, drugs (amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, anticoagulants), crack lung, idiopathic pulmonary haemosiderosis, bone marrow transplant.

Imaging~2 min

What does it look like?

Illustration: CXR showing bilateral airspace shadowing

Illustration: CT with bilateral ground glass and a cavitating nodule (suggests GPA)

Ask the room to call the findings before you reveal them

27:00–29:00. Ask: "What would you report?" Then show the labels.

  • DAH: bilateral, often perihilar or diffuse airspace opacity, with sparing at the periphery and apices. Heart size normal, no effusions (unlike pulmonary oedema).
  • CT: ground glass and consolidation. With repeated bleeds you can see interlobular septal thickening, giving "crazy paving".
  • DAH can clear radiologically within 2–3 days, while pneumonia doesn't.
  • GPA: nodules, often cavitating. MPA: ILD/UIP.

Bronchoscopy~2 min

The tubes that tell the story

  • Sequential aliquots from the same segment that get bloodier each time
  • Haemosiderin-laden macrophages, more than 20% on Perls' stain, appear about 48–72 h after a bleed
  • Just as important, BAL excludes infection: bacteria, PJP PCR, viruses, fungi and TB
Respiratory SpRs: balance the hypoxia risk. Bronchoscopy often happens once the patient is intubated, or not at all. Never delay immunosuppression for it.

29:00–31:00. This is the respiratory team's moment. BAL proves DAH and, crucially, rules out infection before heavy immunosuppression.

In a bleed from an airway (e.g. a tumour), the return gets clearer with each aliquot. In DAH, it gets bloodier.

Practical points: do it early (within 48 h) for the best yield. Discuss with ICU if FiO₂ is high. Send cell count/differential, Perls' stain, cultures, PJP PCR, respiratory viral PCR, fungal culture and AFB.

Section divider. About 42:00.

Acute management~2 min

The first hour: stabilise

A · B

Breathing

  • Titrate oxygen; consider HFNO
  • NIV with caution; bleeding patients deteriorate quickly
  • Early ICU review. Intubate before a crash, not during one
  • Lung-protective ventilation; PEEP may help tamponade

C

Circulation and blood

  • Transfuse to target (locally, often Hb above 70–80)
  • Correct clotting; keep platelets above 50
  • Stop anticoagulants and antiplatelets
  • Two large-bore cannulas; crossmatch

D · E

Kidneys and the rest

  • Treat hyperkalaemia, acidosis and fluid overload
  • Urgent dialysis if indicated
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics until infection is excluded
  • Strict fluid balance chart
Call: renal on-call · ICU outreach · rheumatology/vasculitis team. Don't sit on it overnight.
Start IV methylprednisolone once the picture fits and infection has been reasonably considered. Don't wait for the biopsy.

🫁 Dialysis can stand in for the kidneys for years. Nothing stands in for alveoli. Look after the lungs first.

42:00–44:00. Practical and generic. Emphasise that immunosuppression can start before every result is back if the clinical suspicion is high. This is a joint decision with renal/rheumatology.

In refractory DAH, VV-ECMO has been used successfully as a bridge (case series). It needs specialist centre input.

Induction: AAV~2 min

Induction for ANCA vasculitis

💉

Step 1

Glucocorticoids

IV methylprednisolone 250–1000 mg daily for up to 3 days, then oral prednisolone on a reduced-dose taper (PEXIVAS)

🎯

Step 2

Rituximab or cyclophosphamide

RTX: preferred for relapsing disease, PR3, and when fertility matters.
CYC: IV pulses (CYCLOPS).
Creatinine above about 354 µmol/L: consider RTX + low-dose CYC (KDIGO 2024)

🧬

Step 3

Avacopan

C5aR1 antagonist, 30 mg twice daily. Lets you cut steroids further. Better sustained remission at week 52 (ADVOCATE). NICE recommended (2022)

🔄

Step 4

Plasma exchange?

Not routine. Consider in selected patients: severe kidney injury, DAH with hypoxaemia, or double-positive disease. Next slide: the debate

Then maintenance →

Rituximab every 6 months (MAINRITSAN: better than azathioprine) for 24–48 months after remission (EULAR 2022). Alternatives: azathioprine or methotrexate. Longer for PR3 and relapsing disease.

44:00–46:00. The regimen is decided with renal/rheumatology; your job is to know the principles.

  • Rituximab: 375 mg/m² weekly × 4, or 1 g × 2 two weeks apart.
  • IV cyclophosphamide 15 mg/kg (reduced for age and renal function) at weeks 0, 2, 4, then every 3 weeks (CYCLOPS regimen).
  • Reduced-dose steroids: PEXIVAS showed reduced-dose glucocorticoids were non-inferior for death/ESKD and caused fewer serious infections.
  • Avacopan: in ADVOCATE, it replaced the prednisolone taper. Watch LFTs.

Always check your local and network protocols. Doses here are for teaching.

Live debate~3 min

The great plasma exchange debate

Mr D: creatinine 412 and rising, intubated with DAH, PR3-ANCA positive.

Plasma exchange: yes or no?

Click to add a vote · Shift+click to remove one

PEXIVAS · NEJM 2020 · n = 704

PLEX did not reduce death or ESKD overall. Reduced-dose steroids were non-inferior, with fewer serious infections. About 190 patients had DAH, but few had severe DAH.

Meta-analysis · BMJ 2022 · 9 trials

PLEX probably reduces ESKD at 12 months (more so at higher baseline risk), has little effect on mortality, and may increase serious infections.

What the guidelines say

KDIGO 2024Consider if creatinine >300 µmol/L, on dialysis or rising fast; and for DAH with hypoxaemia
EULAR 2022May be considered if creatinine >300 from active GN; not routinely for DAH
ACR 2021Advises against routine use

46:00–49:00. This is the consultant/SpR discussion slide. Take a vote, then invite one consultant to argue each side for 30 seconds.

Honest summary: there is no definitive trial evidence for PLEX in severe DAH. Guidelines differ, and practice varies between centres. Decisions are individualised. Many centres still use PLEX for life-threatening DAH, and for anti-GBM or double-positive disease (where it is standard).

References: Walsh M et al. NEJM 2020;382:622–31 (PEXIVAS). Walsh M et al. BMJ 2022;376:e064604. KDIGO 2024 ANCA-associated vasculitis guideline. Hellmich B et al. Ann Rheum Dis 2024;83:30–47 (EULAR 2022 update). Chung SA et al. Arthritis Rheumatol 2021 (ACR/VF).

Anti-GBM~1 min

Anti-GBM: remove the antibody, stop making more

🔄

Plasma exchange

Daily, usually about 14 days or until anti-GBM is undetectable. Albumin replacement; give FFP if bleeding or soon after a biopsy.

💉

Steroids

IV methylprednisolone, then prednisolone 1 mg/kg (max 60 mg), tapered over about 6 months.

💊

Cyclophosphamide

Usually oral, 2–3 mg/kg/day for about 3 months (dose reduced for age). Rituximab is sometimes used as an alternative.

Poor renal outlook if dialysis-dependent with 100% crescents, but if there is DAH, treat anyway. The lungs come first.
On the horizon: imlifidase, an enzyme that cleaves IgG in hours. Phase 2 results were promising; phase 3 is under way.

Relapse is rare, so long-term maintenance is usually unnecessary, unless double-positive. Help the patient stop smoking.

49:00–50:00. Anti-GBM disease is where plasma exchange is standard care (KDIGO). Speed matters more than anything: a creatinine above 500 at presentation predicts poor renal recovery.

Imlifidase: GOOD-IDES-01 (phase 2, Uhlin et al. JASN 2022) — single dose in addition to standard care. Phase 3 (GOOD-IDES-02) is ongoing.

Evidence⏩ optional~1 min

Six trials you should know

2009
CYCLOPS

IV pulsed CYC is as good as daily oral CYC for remission, with less leucopenia and slightly more relapse.

2010
RAVE

Rituximab non-inferior to CYC, and superior in relapsing disease. Note: it excluded severe DAH and severe renal disease.

2010
RITUXVAS

RTX + 2 CYC pulses compared with CYC alone in renal AAV: similar outcomes.

2014
MAINRITSAN

Rituximab maintenance beats azathioprine: fewer major relapses.

2020
PEXIVAS

PLEX: no reduction in death or ESKD. Reduced-dose steroids are safe.

2021
ADVOCATE

Avacopan instead of a prednisolone taper: non-inferior at week 26, superior at week 52.

Up-to-date guidance: KDIGO 2024 (AAV) · EULAR 2022 update · BSR guideline for AAV. Local renal network protocols apply.

Optional slide, 50:00–51:00. Skip in the 45-minute version (press S).

For consultants and SpRs: note that RAVE excluded patients with severe DAH (needing mechanical ventilation) and creatinine above 4 mg/dL (about 354 µmol/L). This is why many centres still favour CYC, or RTX + CYC, in the sickest PRS patients.

Don't forget~1 min

The bits that stop the harm

🦠

PJP prophylaxis

Co-trimoxazole (e.g. 480 mg daily). Adjust for renal function.

🦴

Bone protection

Calcium and vitamin D. Consider a bisphosphonate (check eGFR).

🛡️

Gastric protection

PPI while on high-dose steroids.

🩸

VTE

Mechanical while bleeding. Chemical once DAH settles. The risk is genuinely high.

💉

Vaccines

Pneumococcal, influenza, COVID-19. No live vaccines.

🧪

Hep B / HIV

Screen before rituximab. Check immunoglobulins.

👶

Fertility

Discuss sperm or egg preservation before CYC.

🍬

Glucose and BP

Steroid-induced hyperglycaemia. Monitor capillary glucose.

Infection is the leading cause of death in the first year, more often than active vasculitis. Prophylaxis saves lives.

51:00–52:00. This is the slide for FYs and CTs: these are jobs you can make sure happen on the ward round.

In the EUVAS long-term follow-up (Little et al. 2010), infection caused about half of deaths in the first year, compared with about one in five from active vasculitis.

For the chest team~1 min

The respiratory angle

Acute

  • Ventilation: lung-protective; consider higher PEEP
  • VV-ECMO as a bridge in refractory DAH (case series; specialist centre)
  • Tranexamic acid (IV or nebulised): low-quality evidence, sometimes used
  • Intrapulmonary rFVIIa: case reports only, last resort
  • BAL to exclude infection, and repeat if new shadowing appears

Long term: respiratory clinic

  • Subglottic or bronchial stenosis (GPA): flow-volume loops, ENT/airway MDT
  • ILD, especially with MPO-ANCA
  • Bronchiectasis and recurrent infection; hypogammaglobulinaemia after RTX
  • New DAH = relapse until proven otherwise
  • Nodules: follow up, and remember TB and malignancy

52:00–53:00. Tailored to the pulmonary SpRs and consultants. Ask: "How many vasculitis patients are in our ILD or bronchiectasis clinics?" Probably more than we think.

Case 2 · team quiz~1 min
Ms K, 24, smokes 15 a day. Two days of haemoptysis, SpO₂ 90% on air, creatinine 680. Anti-GBM strongly positive, ANCA negative. Biopsy: linear IgG, 85% crescents. What's next?
B. Anti-GBM disease with DAH is an emergency: daily plasma exchange, steroids and cyclophosphamide. Even if the kidneys may not recover, the lung haemorrhage must be treated, so D is wrong. Smoking cessation too.

Prognosis⏩ optional~1 min

What happens next?

Bad signs

  • Severe DAH needing ventilation
  • Dialysis at presentation
  • Older age
  • Few normal glomeruli on biopsy
  • Five-Factor Score: age >65, cardiac or GI involvement, creatinine ≥150, no ENT disease

Relapse

  • Common in AAV, especially PR3
  • Can present as new DAH, haematuria or ENT symptoms
  • Rare in anti-GBM disease
  • Track activity with BVAS and damage with VDI

Long-term burden

  • CKD and ESKD
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Infection and hypogammaglobulinaemia
  • Cancer (bladder after CYC, skin)
  • Fatigue: it's real
With modern treatment, most patients with AAV survive to 5 years. The challenge now is preventing damage, toxicity and relapse.

Optional slide. Skip in the 45-minute version.

FFS 2009 (Guillevin): age >65, cardiac insufficiency, GI involvement, renal insufficiency (creatinine ≥150 µmol/L) and absence of ENT manifestations (for GPA/EGPA).

Myth or fact?⏩ optional~2 min

Myth or fact?

"No haemoptysis, so it can't be DAH."

Click to flip
MYTH

Up to a third have no haemoptysis. Follow the Hb and the oxygen requirement.

"You need a renal biopsy before you start steroids."

Click to flip
MYTH

If the picture and serology fit, treat. The biopsy is still informative several days later.

"Rituximab is as good as cyclophosphamide for induction in AAV."

Click to flip
FACT

RAVE: non-inferior, and better in relapsing disease. (Severe PRS was excluded.)

"A rising ANCA titre on its own means you should escalate treatment."

Click to flip
MYTH

It's a modest predictor of relapse. Watch closely, but treat the patient, not the number.

"Anti-GBM disease relapses as often as ANCA vasculitis."

Click to flip
MYTH

Anti-GBM disease is usually monophasic. Double-positive patients are the exception.

"Infection kills more patients than vasculitis in the first year."

Click to flip
FACT

So prescribe PJP prophylaxis, screen before rituximab, and use the lowest effective steroid dose.

Question 1 of 4
Which trial showed rituximab was non-inferior to cyclophosphamide for induction in ANCA vasculitis?
RAVE (Stone et al., NEJM 2010). Rituximab was non-inferior overall and superior in relapsing disease.

About 30 seconds per question. The first team to shout the right answer gets the point.

Question 2 of 4
Avacopan works by blocking…
B: C5aR1. C5a primes and recruits neutrophils (remember the pathophysiology slide). CD20 is rituximab, IL-5 is mepolizumab (used in EGPA), BAFF is belimumab.

This links back to the ANCA pathogenesis diagram. Bonus point for anyone who names the drug for each wrong option.

Question 3 of 4
Before giving rituximab, which test is essential?
A. Rituximab can reactivate hepatitis B, which can be fatal. Check HBsAg and anti-HBc. Also check HIV, hepatitis C and baseline immunoglobulins.

This one is for FYs and CTs. Encourage them to answer first.

Question 4 of 4 · double points
In PEXIVAS, what did adding plasma exchange do to the primary outcome (death or ESKD)?
B. No significant reduction in death or ESKD. Its most practice-changing finding was that reduced-dose steroids were just as effective, with fewer serious infections.
